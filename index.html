A simulated racing drone that flies through gates by camera, with no GPS or map

Pooled over 300 seeded courses, it passes 99.8% of gates in a clean world and 98.6% with gate-coloured clutter beside the course. There is no GPS, depth sensor or map of the gate positions: it finds each gate in its camera image, estimates its own position relative to the gate and flies through.

Approach

  1. Detect. Threshold the gate colour and fit a four-cornered outline to each gate and to its opening.
  2. Measure. The gate's known size and its four image corners give its pose relative to the camera, using OpenCV solvePnP with IPPE_SQUARE. Gates stand upright, so the pose is then refined for position and yaw only, with the up direction taken from gravity.
  3. Fuse. An error-state Kalman filter tracks position, velocity and attitude in the frame of the next gate, so no map is needed. A chi-squared test rejects outlier detections.
  4. Fly. Guidance aims through the gate centre and is converted into thrust and body-rate commands, the way an FPV pilot flies in acro mode.

How it was built

I set the problem and the design decisions that stay fixed: acro thrust and body-rate commands, identical gates with no IDs, a strict wall between the simulator and the flight stack, and the benchmark rules. Most of the code was written by an AI coding agent (Claude) in scheduled overnight runs. Each night it tried one change behind a flag, flew the same seeded courses with it on and off, and kept it only if it clearly helped. I reviewed the results and worked through some changes with it directly.

Testing

A deterministic regression harness flies every change on the same seeded courses in lockstep (also over UDP, with identical results) and reports the gate pass rate. On the first set of 50 courses the pass rate rose from 89.2% at the first committed baseline to 100%. Because one set moves by a few points from noise alone, a change is kept only if it helps pooled over 300 courses, where the pass rate reached 99.8%.

With that benchmark saturated, the world was made harder: orange panels and cones beside the course, in the gate's own colour. Pooled over the same 300 courses the pass rate is now 98.6% with the IMU and 97.5% camera only. Course 3 in the replay is one of the failures: with the IMU, the drone twice takes a distractor for the gate, and the pipeline strip shows its filter rejecting measurements and re-initialising before it misses gate 2.

One bug came from the simulator itself: its sign convention was the reverse of what its documentation said. I found it by comparing commanded inputs against logged attitude, confirmed it with single-axis tests, and corrected the controller.

Single sets of 50 courses move by a few points from noise alone, so the pooled row is the one to compare. "Camera only" drops the IMU and predicts motion between frames from the drone's own commands. The clutter is up to 20 flat panels and cones beside the course, painted within the gate's colour range. That world is harder by design, so its numbers start a new baseline rather than continuing the clean one. Clean: runs 20 and 23 (camera only); clutter: run 26.

A generatively designed 3-inch FPV drone frame

A 38 g printed frame, 36% lighter than a comparable plate frame, that keeps the complete drone at 246 g, under the UK CAA's 250 g threshold. Arm deflection measured at maximum thrust agreed with Ansys to within 18%.

Safety factor1.5
First mode, required>300Hz
Mass vs plate frame−36%
Printed frame38g

Approach

I derived eight operational load cases, including maximum thrust, arm collision and battery impact, to define the design envelope and starting geometry.

I then ran Fusion's generative optimisation under those loads with buckling, modal frequency (above 300 Hz) and 1.5 safety-factor constraints. The selected topology is 36% lighter than a comparable plate-based frame.

Build and testing

I printed the frame in PC/ABS for impact toughness. It weighs 38 g, and the fully assembled drone comes to 246 g, under the 250 g threshold.

I measured arm deflection at maximum thrust and compared it with Ansys Mechanical; the two agreed to within 18%.

A pipe-climbing robot with its drivetrain chosen by simulation

The robot completed the climb at the IMechE Design Challenge. I built the team's Excel model of the drivetrain and used it to pick the setup from more than 20 simulated: it cut predicted climb time by 22%, and real runs measured 16%. The brief: a 2 m pipe, dragging a chain, inside 400 × 300 mm, on £50, within 3 minutes.

Pipe height2m
Envelope400×300mm
Setups simulated20+
Measured climb time−16%

Approach

I built the team's dynamic performance model in Excel. It combines the motor and vehicle equations of motion and integrates them with explicit Euler at a 10 ms time step. Each step checks for slip, so the model only accepts a setup if the wheels have enough friction to carry the drive force.

I used it to simulate more than 20 combinations of gear ratio, motor torque, wheel radius and normal force, and picked the drivetrain with the fastest predicted climb.

Result

At the IMechE Design Challenge the robot completed the climb.

The chosen setup cut predicted climb time by 22%. In real runs it cut climb time by 16%.

I also produced the full Fusion CAD assembly across two design iterations, including a working geartrain.

Limitations

Looking back, the sheet's 10 ms step was too coarse for the drive's time constant of about 3 ms, and its speed and torque columns used different gear ratios (100 and 150). Fig. 04 corrects both, with a 1 ms step and one ratio, so it is a demonstration rather than the coursework's numbers.

How AI training power scales with model size

Statistical analysis of AI training data, 358 models across nine domains in the coursework, used to fit a scaling law and estimate the training power of a frontier model.

Models (coursework)358
Fitted lawP ∝ N0.55
Fit R²0.44
GPT-5 estimate≈590kW

The data was heavily right-skewed, so I log₁₀-transformed it to make the nine domains comparable, then fitted a power law of training power against parameter count; higher-order fits gave no meaningful improvement or overfitted. Parameter count explains a little under half the variation. I used the fit to estimate GPT-5's training power draw and wrote a report on the social, environmental and economic effects of rapid AI training. With R² this low, and GPT-5's parameter count not public, that estimate is an order of magnitude rather than a prediction.

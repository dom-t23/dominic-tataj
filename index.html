A simulated racing drone that flies through gates using only its onboard camera

There is no GPS, depth sensor or map of the gate positions. The drone detects each gate in its camera image, estimates its position relative to the gate and flies through.

Approach

  1. Detect. Threshold the gate colour and fit a four-cornered outline to each gate and to its opening.
  2. Measure. The gate's known size and its four image corners give its pose relative to the camera, using OpenCV solvePnP with IPPE_SQUARE. Gates stand upright, so the pose is then refined for position and yaw only, with the up direction taken from gravity.
  3. Fuse. An error-state Kalman filter tracks position, velocity and attitude in the frame of the next gate, so no map is needed. A chi-squared test rejects outlier detections.
  4. Fly. Guidance aims through the gate centre and is converted into thrust and body-rate commands, the way an FPV pilot flies in acro mode.

Testing

I built a deterministic regression harness, with lockstep simulation over UDP, which flies every change on the same seeded courses and reports the gate pass rate. On the first set of 50 courses the pass rate rose from 89.2% at the first committed baseline to 100%. Because one set moves by a few points from noise alone, a change is kept only if it helps pooled over 300 courses, where the pass rate now stands at 99.5%.

One bug came from the simulator itself: its sign convention was the reverse of what its documentation said. I found it by comparing commanded inputs against logged attitude, confirmed it with single-axis tests, and corrected the controller.

Single sets of 50 courses move by a few points from noise alone, so the pooled row is the one to compare. "Camera only" drops the IMU and predicts motion between frames from the drone's own commands.

A generatively designed 3-inch FPV drone frame

The frame needed to survive crashes and avoid resonance with the motors, while keeping the complete drone under the UK CAA's 250 g threshold.

Load cases8
Safety factor1.5
Modal constraint>300Hz
Vs plate design−36%mass
Printed frame38g
FEA agreement≤18%

Approach

I derived eight operational load cases, including maximum thrust, arm collision and battery impact, to define the design envelope and starting geometry.

I then ran Fusion's generative optimisation under those loads with buckling, modal frequency (above 300 Hz) and 1.5 safety-factor constraints. The selected topology is 36% lighter than a comparable plate-based frame.

Build and testing

I printed the frame in PC/ABS for impact toughness. It weighs 38 g, and the fully assembled drone comes to 246 g, under the 250 g threshold.

I measured arm deflection at maximum thrust and compared it with Ansys Mechanical; the two agreed to within 18%.

A pipe-climbing robot with its drivetrain chosen by simulation

The robot had to climb a 2 m pipe while dragging a chain whose load grows as it climbs, inside a 400 × 300 mm envelope, on a £50 budget, within 3 minutes.

Pipe height2m
Envelope400×300mm
Budget£50
Time step10ms
Setups simulated20+
Measured gain−16%time

Approach

I built the team's dynamic performance model in Excel. It combines the motor and vehicle equations of motion and integrates them with explicit Euler at a 10 ms time step. Each step checks for slip, so the model only accepts a setup if the wheels have enough friction to carry the drive force.

I used it to simulate more than 20 combinations of gear ratio, motor torque, wheel radius and normal force, and picked the drivetrain with the fastest predicted climb.

Result

The chosen setup cut predicted climb time by 22%. In real runs it cut climb time by 16%.

I also produced the full Fusion CAD assembly across two design iterations, including a working geartrain.

How AI training power scales with model size

Statistical analysis of training data for 358 AI models across nine domains, used to fit a scaling law and estimate the training power of a frontier model.

Approach

I summarised each domain with mean, median, standard deviation, skewness and quartiles. The data was heavily right-skewed, with skewness up to 6.1.

A log₁₀ transformation cut skewness to below 0.9, which made the nine domains comparable and the boxplots readable.

I fitted a power law of training power against parameter count. Higher-order fits gave no meaningful improvement or overfitted, so I kept the simpler model.

Result

The fit gives P ∝ N0.55 with R² = 0.44, so parameter count explains a little under half the variation in training power. I used it to estimate GPT-5's training power draw at about 590 kW, and wrote a report on the social, environmental and economic effects of rapid AI training.

With R² this low, and GPT-5's parameter count not public, the estimate is an order of magnitude rather than a prediction.
